## Specs
- Product type: Portable fire extinguisher
- Agent: HCFC (hydrochlorofluorocarbon)
- Sizes on the masterlist: 5 lb, 10 lb, 20 lb
- Mounting: Wall type, with a wall bracket (5 lb) or a wall hook (10 lb and 20 lb)

## Usage
These are general facts for this type of extinguisher, not the datasheet of CTI's unit. HCFC agents belong to the halocarbon group of halogenated agents. The U.S. EPA accepts these halocarbons as substitutes for halons. Halogenated extinguishers typically carry listings for Class A, B and C fires, depending on size. Units under 9.5 lb carry no Class A listing, but they still work on those fires.

## Advantages
- NFPA 10 defines halogenated agents as electrically non-conducting.
- They leave no residue when they evaporate. Dry chemical powder, by contrast, stays on surfaces and can harm electronics.
- NFPA 10 advises carbon dioxide or halogenated extinguishers for delicate electronics such as computers.

## Cautions
- HCFC agents have an ozone depletion potential above zero. The U.S. EPA lists 0.02 for HCFC-123.
- The U.S. EPA SNAP program accepts HCFC Blend B and HCFC-123 streaming agents for non-residential uses only.
- Under the Montreal Protocol, developing countries must cut HCFC consumption to zero from 2030. A small allowance for servicing existing fire protection equipment runs to 2040.
- UL sets a minimum room volume for each clean agent extinguisher rating. More toxic agents need larger rooms.
- One maker's table rates Halotron I, an HCFC agent based on HCFC-123, as more toxic than HFC-236fa.
